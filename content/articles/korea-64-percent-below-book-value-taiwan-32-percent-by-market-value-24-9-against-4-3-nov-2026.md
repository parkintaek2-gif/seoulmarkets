Two ways of counting, one finding. By number of companies, 64.6% of Korean listed companies
with a price-to-book figure trade below book value; in Taiwan the share is 32.0%. By market
value, the Korean companies below book are 24.9% of the total and the Taiwanese ones 4.3%.

We built both counts from the tapes we hold: the Korea Valuation Tape, priced on 25 November,
and the Taiwan financials tape, priced on 26 November. Book values come from different periods,
and that matters for how far the gap can be trusted. It is set out below.

## The two distributions side by side

| Price-to-book | Korea (2,539) | Taiwan (1,088) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Below 0.5 | 947 (37.3%) | 50 (4.6%) |
| 0.5 to under 1.0 | 693 (27.3%) | 298 (27.4%) |
| 1.0 to under 2.0 | 457 (18.0%) | 416 (38.2%) |
| 2.0 to under 3.0 | 155 (6.1%) | 162 (14.9%) |
| 3.0 to under 5.0 | 114 (4.5%) | 85 (7.8%) |
| 5.0 and above | 173 (6.8%) | 77 (7.1%) |
| Below 1.0, total | 1,640 (64.6%) | 348 (32.0%) |
| Median | 0.68 | 1.36 |

The difference is almost entirely in the first row. In Korea 947 companies, 37.3% of those with
a figure, trade at less than half of book. In Taiwan 50 do. The band from 0.5 to 1.0 is the same
size in both, 27% of companies, and the top tail at 5.0 and above is also about the same, near
7%.

So the two markets look alike at the top and in the middle-low band, and differ sharply in the
deepest discount band. That is more specific than saying one market is cheaper.

## Count against value

Count the companies and Korea has roughly twice Taiwan's share below book. Weight by market
value and the gap is wider: 24.9% against 4.3%.

The direction is the same as the count, and the size is different. In Korea, a quarter of the
combined market value sits in companies priced under their stated equity. In Taiwan it is
under one-twentieth. Taiwan's largest company, TSMC, is priced at 7.95 times book and is on its
own a larger weight in the total than the whole below-book group.

## Inside Korea

The Korean tape marks each company with a market code. The first market, which includes Samsung
Electronics, has 809 companies with a figure, and 614 of them, 75.9%, are below book. The second
market has 1,721, and 1,023, 59.4%, are below book. Nine more sit under neither code, and three of
those are below book.

The first market is therefore more often below book by count than the second, 75.9% against
59.4%. We counted; we did not test why.

Both markets share one feature: heavy industry and older consumer-facing businesses sit lowest,
and research-led businesses sit highest. In Taiwan, plastics (18 of 21 below book) and
textiles (29 of 42) are at the bottom and semiconductors (5 of 96) at the top. In Korea,
primary metals (78 of 85) and motor vehicles (95 of 106) are at the bottom and research and
development (10 of 63) at the top.

The industry names on the two tapes are not the same classification, so an industry in one is not
a clean match for one in the other. The point here is only the order within each market, not a
pairing between them.

## Why the gap cannot be read as a verdict

Three things make this comparison softer than the table suggests.

The book values are from different dates. Korean book value is total equity from fiscal 2025
annual reports. Taiwanese book value is from the third quarter of 2026. A Korean company that
lost money or paid out a lot since its annual report reads higher than it should, and one that
earned a lot reads lower. Taiwan's figures are at most one quarter old.

The populations are different. Taiwan's tape covers the main-board Taiwan Stock Exchange only; the
Taipei Exchange is not in it. Korea's covers its two main boards. The rules that decide who
lists differ, so who is counted differs before any valuation question arises.

And Japan is missing. The Japanese tape has no price-to-book figure for any of its 3,708
companies. For 3,142 of them the tape records the reason as the filing not stating which basis
the book value is struck on; for the other 566 it records none. We did not produce a figure from
a price we reconstructed. A third column would be a made-up one.

## What we are not saying

We are not saying that Korean shares are cheap or that Taiwanese shares are expensive. A ratio
under 1.0 can reflect a discount, or can reflect a market that expects the assets to earn very
little. The ratio measures a price against an accounting number; it does not say which of those
it is.

What the tapes do show is a size. Of Korean companies with a figure, 947 trade below half of book.
Of Taiwan's, 50 do. That is the single widest difference between the two distributions.
